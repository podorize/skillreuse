# 6. Conclusion

We asked whether the text of an agent skill predicts whether other developers will copy it. To measure copying reliably, we dated the copies of 150,000 GitSkills skills and counted, for each skill, the copies made by other developers in the 60 days after it first appeared, leaving aside copies by the same account and repositories that collect skills in bulk. A logistic regression on 22 attributes of the text reached an AUC of 0.645 on skills from authors it had never seen. The text said more than its length and more than the origin repository, popularity included, and the result held under other windows, other definitions of aggregator and a split in time. The signal came above all from writing style: skills written as explanatory prose, with a stable vocabulary and valid metadata, were copied more often. The relation is modest and associative. It does not say what makes a skill good, only which kinds of skills tend to travel.

The study opens several directions. Grouping near-identical copies would capture skills that are adapted rather than copied verbatim, and a longer follow-up would show whether early copying leads to lasting adoption. Information about who wrote a skill, with which tool, and whether it is distributed by an installer would show how much of the style effect reflects provenance. Finally, richer representations of the text, and experiments that change how a skill is written, would move from association toward the question of what makes instructions for agents worth reusing.
