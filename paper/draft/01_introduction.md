# 1. Introduction

## 1.1 Context and motivation

Agent skills are portable directories that combine structured metadata, natural-language instructions, and optional scripts and reference materials. At their core, a `SKILL.md` file specifies how an LLM-based agent should perform a task, including the constraints it must follow and the resources it may use \cite{agentskills_spec}. Although software reuse has been extensively studied \cite{haefliger2008code,lopes2017dejavu}, agent skills introduce distinct challenges because their distribution does not require a centralized registry or package management system. Instead, skills can be replicated by copying their directories across repositories. However, the presence of identical skill content in multiple repositories does not necessarily indicate independent adoption. Such replication may reflect characteristics of the skill itself, such as clarity and portability, or external factors, including its inclusion in templates or catalogs, automated installation by development tools, and duplication across projects maintained by the same account. Consequently, observed replication patterns reflect a combination of artifact characteristics and distribution contexts, making it difficult to distinguish potentially meaningful reuse from other forms of duplication.

The GitSkills dataset provides an opportunity to investigate these patterns at scale by documenting skill occurrences, distinct contents, and associated repository metadata \cite{gitskills2027}. Previous research has examined defects in skill specifications \cite{zhang2026keepskillsreuse}, the effectiveness of skills in software engineering tasks \cite{han2026sweskillsbench}, and the evolution of prompts in software repositories \cite{tafreshipour2025prompting}. Building on this literature, the present study investigates whether observable characteristics of agent skills and their repositories are associated with replication across distinct GitHub owners. It also examines a related methodological concern: whether the procedures used to construct the dataset introduce systematic biases that influence the apparent predictability of such replication.

## 1.2 Research problem

This study investigates **cross-owner replication**, defined as the presence of identical `SKILL.md` content, identified by its `content_hash`, in repositories belonging to at least two distinct GitHub owners. Each distinct content constitutes one observation, and an owner is defined as the account identified by the prefix of a repository's full name. The primary prediction target, `cross_owner`, indicates whether a content appears under two or more owners, while the secondary target, `owner_count`, measures the number of distinct owners associated with that content. Although counting owners provides a more meaningful approximation of diffusion than counting repositories, it does not establish independent adoption: multiple accounts may belong to the same individual or organization, and identical content may be propagated through templates, catalogs, or automated installation tools.

The analysis is restricted to contents observed in at least two repositories (`repository_count >= 2`). Within this population, the objective is to distinguish replication across owners from duplication within a single owner's repositories, rather than to predict whether an arbitrary new skill will ever be reused. This restriction also explains the high prevalence of cross-owner replication: 75.3% in the full eligible population, and about 80% in the capped development sample and the holdout, where the sampling protocol raises it further. Moreover, the study adopts a cross-sectional predictive design: both the attributes and the targets are derived from the same July 2026 repository snapshot. Consequently, repository characteristics such as stars, forks, and last-push dates may have been observed after replication occurred. The models therefore assess associations with observed replication rather than predicting future adoption. Accordingly, the findings are interpreted as predictive associations, without claims of causality or skill quality \cite{shmueli2010explain}.

Two methodological challenges further complicate the analysis. **The first concerns the measurement of reuse.** The number of repositories containing identical content (`repository_count`) may overstate diffusion because it includes copies maintained by the same owner. In the full population of contents appearing in at least two repositories, 24.7% occur exclusively under a single owner, a proportion that reaches 28.0% in the development set. Furthermore, one account accounts for approximately 12% of the development observations. These patterns indicate that repository counts conflate cross-owner replication with within-owner duplication, motivating the use of owner-based targets.

**The second challenge concerns the construction of predictive attributes.** GitSkills retains detailed metadata for only one representative occurrence of each distinct content. This representative is selected by prioritizing certain directory locations, particularly `.claude/skills/`, followed by a deterministic alphabetical ordering of repositories and paths. As the number of occurrences increases, so does the likelihood that at least one copy resides in a preferred location. Consequently, the representative's location class, along with the availability of commit-history information associated with that location, can encode information about the number of copies through the selection procedure itself. Such attributes may therefore produce misleading predictive signals unrelated to the characteristics that explain replication. To mitigate this problem, the main models exclude the identified construction-dependent variables, while their effects are examined separately. Nevertheless, the remaining repository and path attributes still describe the selected representative, so the exclusion does not eliminate every potential effect of the selection procedure.

Together, these challenges motivate a predictive framework that distinguishes cross-owner replication from within-owner duplication while accounting for identifiable dataset-construction biases. The evaluation combines owner-grouped validation, controls for owner concentration, and a separate holdout disjoint by representative owner. Because the research hypotheses and evaluation protocol were finalized after an initial exploratory phase, only the single evaluation of the frozen models on the new holdout is treated as confirmatory; results obtained during development remain exploratory.

## 1.3 Objectives and research questions

* **RQ1 — Construction biases:** to what extent do the representative-selection rule, self-copying and owner concentration confound the target and the attributes of GitSkills?
* **RQ2 — Predictability:** after excluding the identified construction-dependent shortcuts, can cross-owner reuse and the number of owners be predicted better than baselines, and is the estimate confirmed on a holdout disjoint by representative owner?
* **RQ3 — Origin of the signal:** what is the contribution of skill, repository and catalog attributes, which variables are associated with the predictions, and in which direction?

## 1.4 Contributions

(i) An **audit of the GitSkills representative**: we recover its rule (97.2% agreement) and show that location and history depend on the number of copies. (ii) A **protocol** for cross-owner reuse with an owner-based target, a per-owner cap, grouped validation, explicit hypotheses and confirmation on a new holdout. (iii) Evidence of a **modest predictive signal**, with a stable RMSLE gain across protocol variations, confirmed on unseen data (AUC 0.73) and decomposed into blocks by one-block ablations (exploratory). (iv) A **reproducible package** with the data revision, seeds, a five-minute reproduction path and a lock that prevents reusing the holdout.
