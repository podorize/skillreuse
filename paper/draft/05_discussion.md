# 5. Discussion

## 5.1 What the signal means

Observable attributes of a skill and of its repository carry information about cross-owner reuse, but not much: an AUC of 0.73 on the holdout and a 6% RMSLE reduction relative to the mean of `log1p`. The result holds across three layers of evidence (nested validation, development robustness and an owner-disjoint holdout), but it is a signal of **coarse ordering**: the Spearman of the regression model is 0.33, and at the threshold that maximizes balanced accuracy the model recovers 79% of single-owner contents with a precision of 28%.

The block decomposition answers RQ3: skill (file structure) and repository (visibility and activity) contribute complementarily (AUC 0.65–0.67 each, 0.72 together). Permutation importance and partial dependence associate cross-owner reuse mainly with **stars and forks** and with repositories whose **last push is old**, which is consistent with skills in more visible repositories, and in repositories that have been inactive for longer, being copied by other owners. The association admits more than one explanation (visibility that generates copies, accumulated exposure time, or repositories valued for containing skills that were already copied), and the data do not separate them. In the regression, what stands out is the **absence of a license** and, for large catalogs, the number of skills in the repository; the mechanism of the first is unknown (Section 4.5).

**Cross-sectional, not prospective.** Attributes and labels come from the same July 2026 snapshot. Stars, forks and last-push date were measured after some or all observed copying could have occurred, so the association with stars and forks may reflect copies that raised visibility as much as visibility that attracted copies, and an old last push may reflect accumulated exposure time. The results therefore show that attributes discriminate between skills with different levels of observed replication; they do not show that these attributes would have predicted reuse before it happened. This is not target leakage in the conventional sense (no attribute is computed from the target), but it separates our problem from prospective prediction, which would require attributes dated before the copies appear.

## 5.2 Implications for using GitSkills

The most solid contribution is methodological and answers RQ1. (1) **`location_class` and `history_fetched` are not legitimate predictors of reuse**: by construction, the representative's class grows with the number of copies (14% → 92%) and history inherits the dependence; including them makes the "repository" view look much more informative than it is (Section 4.2). (2) **Counting repositories mixes diffusion with self-copying**; the number of owners is a more faithful target (a quarter of the reused contents have a single owner). (3) **Results depend on a few accounts**: the AUC varied from 0.615 to 0.747 with the treatment of owners with 500 to 1000 contents, so sensitivity to concentrated owners, or a cap per owner, should be standard practice. (4) **The representative describes an arbitrary copy**: among copies of the same class the choice is alphabetical, so repository attributes are those of one repository among several. Aggregates over all copies would be more informative but derive from the same occurrences that define the target; they should be treated as leakage in predictive studies, or computed only from information prior to reuse.

## 5.3 Models and data limits

The six families are statistically indistinguishable in both tasks, and the hyperparameter search did not help (default RF 0.716 against 0.701 searched). The linear models trail the forests by 0.02–0.04 AUC even after `log1p`, but with intervals that include zero: the evidence is of a tie within the resolution of 6 outer folds, not of proven equivalence. When families with such different inductive biases reach similar performance, the limit usually lies in the data: the attributes describe one copy, and the target is an exact count of hashes that ignores modified copies. Relevant gains will probably require new information (relations among copies, time), not more flexible models.

## 5.4 Threats to validity and limitations

**Construct and temporal.** The study is cross-sectional (Section 5.1): it does not assess prospective prediction of future reuse. The number of owners approximates independent adoption, but accounts may represent the same person or organization, and GitHub search counts forks only if they have more stars than the parent. Counts are lower bounds, the exact hash misses variants, and reuse by copying does not indicate use, quality or usefulness.

**Internal.** (i) H1–H4 were fixed after exploratory results; only the holdout is confirmatory. (ii) The cap of 50 per owner is data-dependent. (iii) Tuning used 2 repetitions of 3 folds and 6 candidates, fewer than planned. (iv) With 9 non-independent scores, the Nadeau–Bengio test is approximate, and Holm p-values depend on the family size. (v) **Residual selection effects:** excluding the shortcuts does not remove the influence of the representative rule, which still decides which repository supplies stars, forks, license, last push and the catalog and path attributes, and the number of copies may influence that selection; a robustness check with a representative chosen independently of location would require attributes of alternative copies and was not feasible here. The catalog and path blocks may be partly affected by the preference for standard locations; that the path barely helps and that the signal persists within each location class reduces, but does not eliminate, this risk. (vi) The threshold analysis is descriptive: it uses the saved holdout predictions.

**External.** The reported AUC is performance under the owner-balanced protocol (Section 3.6), not an unrestricted ecosystem-wide estimate: the cap of 50 in development was chosen after seeing the AUC's sensitivity (0.615 to 0.747), and the holdout contains no concentrated owners (at most 37 contents each), so the result does not generalize to heavily concentrated owners, whose behavior may differ. Inverse-owner-frequency weighting, instead of a cap, is a natural comparison for future work. The corpus is a July 2026 snapshot of public repositories. The holdout excludes concentrated owners by construction and has more `canonical` skills (36% against 21%) than development. The original test partition was used in exploratory analyses and supports no confirmatory claim. Near-identical variants of the same skill may still lie on both sides of the split.

**Interpretation and ethics.** Permutation importance and partial dependence are associative, depend on the model and vary across folds; nothing here establishes causality. The data are public and GitSkills anonymizes commit authors; we omit owner identifiers, and the analysis does not aim to evaluate individuals.
