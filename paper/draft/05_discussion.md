# 5. Discussion

## 5.1 What the signal means

Observable attributes of a skill and of its repository carry information about cross-owner reuse, but not much: an AUC of 0.73 on the holdout and a 6% RMSLE reduction relative to the mean of `log1p`. The result holds across nested validation, development robustness and the holdout, but it is a signal of **coarse ordering**: the Spearman of the regression model is 0.33, and at the threshold that maximizes balanced accuracy the model recovers 79% of single-owner contents with a precision of 28%.

The block analysis answers RQ3 with caution: skill (file structure) and repository (visibility and activity) carry complementary information for classification (AUC 0.65–0.67 each, 0.72 together; removing either from the full set lowers the AUC by 0.04–0.05), whereas catalog and path add little, and in regression the contributions are small and uneven (Section 4.3). Permutation importance and partial dependence associate cross-owner reuse mainly with **stars and forks** (stars give the largest permutation-importance decrease in the evaluated model, but the two are correlated and their individual shares are not separable) and with repositories whose **last push is old**, which is consistent with skills in more visible repositories, and in repositories that have been inactive for longer, being copied by other owners. The association admits more than one explanation (visibility that generates copies, accumulated exposure time, or repositories valued for containing skills that were already copied), and the data do not separate them. In the regression, what stands out is the **absence of a license** and, for large catalogs, the number of skills in the repository; the license result is an exploratory finding with unknown mechanism (Section 4.5), not a general explanation of reuse.

**Cross-sectional, not prospective.** Stars, forks and last-push date were measured after some or all observed copying could have occurred, so the association with stars and forks may reflect copies that raised visibility as much as visibility that attracted copies, and an old last push may reflect accumulated exposure time. This is not target leakage in the conventional sense, but the results do not show that these attributes would have predicted reuse before it happened (Section 1.2).

## 5.2 Implications for using GitSkills

The most solid contribution is methodological and answers RQ1. (1) **`location_class` and `history_fetched` are not legitimate predictors of reuse**: by construction, the representative's class grows with the number of copies (14% → 92%) and history inherits the dependence; including them makes the "repository" view look much more informative than it is (Section 4.2). (2) **Counting repositories mixes diffusion with self-copying**; the number of owners is a more faithful target (a quarter of the reused contents have a single owner). (3) **Results depend on a few accounts**: the AUC varied from 0.615 to 0.747 with the treatment of owners with 500 to 1000 contents, so sensitivity to concentrated owners, or a cap per owner, should be standard practice. (4) **The representative describes an arbitrary copy**: among copies of the same class the choice is alphabetical, so repository attributes are those of one repository among several. Aggregates over all copies derive from the occurrences that define the target and should be avoided, or computed only from information prior to reuse.

## 5.3 Models and data limits

Under the families and the tuning budget evaluated (6 outer folds, 6 candidates per search, reduced for computational cost), the six families are not statistically separated in either task, apart from the tree and, less clearly, KNN, and the hyperparameter search did not help (default RF 0.716 against 0.701 searched). The linear models trail the forests by 0.02–0.04 AUC even after `log1p`, with intervals that include zero: this is a tie within the resolution of 6 outer folds, not proven equivalence. Increasing model flexibility did not consistently improve performance, but the data do not show that substantially better performance is impossible with more careful model selection, other architectures or alternative representations. A plausible reading is that the attributes describe one copy and the target is an exact count of hashes that ignores modified copies, so new information (relations among copies, time, richer representations) may be more promising than more flexible models; this remains a hypothesis.

## 5.4 Threats to validity and limitations

**Construct.** The number of owners approximates independent adoption only partly: it removes duplication by one account, but templates, catalogs and installation tools can still generate replication among owners. Forks are not a concern in these data (an audit found 8 occurrences in 3 fork repositories out of 3,797,117, none changing a `cross_owner` label; `results/owner/fork_audit.json`). Accounts may represent the same person or organization. Counts are lower bounds, the exact hash misses variants, and reuse by copying does not indicate use, quality or usefulness.

**Internal.** (i) H1–H4 were fixed after exploratory results; only the holdout is confirmatory. (ii) The cap of 50 per owner is data-dependent. (iii) Tuning used 2 repetitions of 3 folds and 6 candidates, fewer than planned. (iv) With 9 non-independent scores, the Nadeau–Bengio test is approximate, and Holm p-values depend on the family size. (v) **Residual selection effects:** excluding the shortcuts does not remove the influence of the representative rule, which still decides which repository supplies stars, forks, license, last push and the catalog and path attributes, and the number of copies may influence that selection; a check with a representative chosen independently of location would require attributes of alternative copies and was not feasible here. That the path barely helps and that the signal persists within each location class reduces, but does not eliminate, this risk. (vi) The threshold analysis is descriptive: it uses the saved holdout predictions. (vii) **Owner separation is partial.** Validation and holdout separate representative owners, but a content can appear under several owners: in 73% of the validation rows at least one occurrence owner also appears among the occurrence owners of the training contents, and 51% of the holdout contents have an occurrence owner among the representative owners of the development contents (68% among all their occurrence owners). The models use no owner identifier, but the holdout does not test generalization to completely unseen owners.

**External.** The population is contents with at least two repositories, so the results do not apply to whether a skill is reused at all. The reported AUC is performance under the owner-capped protocol (Section 3.6), not an unrestricted ecosystem-wide estimate: the cap of 50 was chosen after seeing the AUC's sensitivity (0.615 to 0.747), and the holdout has no concentrated owners (at most 37 contents each) and more `canonical` skills (36% against 21%) than development, so the result does not extend to heavily concentrated owners. The corpus is a July 2026 snapshot of public repositories. The original test partition was used in exploratory analyses and supports no confirmatory claim. Near-identical variants of the same skill may still lie on both sides of the split.

**Interpretation and ethics.** Permutation importance and partial dependence are associative; correlation among attributes (stars and forks, several structure counts) can distribute importance unpredictably, and partial dependence can evaluate rare combinations, so strongest-feature statements are specific to the evaluated model and data distribution. They vary across folds; nothing here establishes causality. The data are public and GitSkills anonymizes commit authors; we omit owner identifiers, and the analysis does not aim to evaluate individuals.
