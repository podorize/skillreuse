# 2. Related work

## 2.1 Reuse, cloning and natural-language artifacts

Software reuse is studied as component adoption and as the dissemination of solutions across open ecosystems \cite{mockus2002two,haefliger2008code}, and large-scale cloning on GitHub shows that duplicated content is common \cite{lopes2017dejavu}. Reuse may occur through formal dependencies, component copying, templates and loosely standardized processes \cite{heinemann2011extents}. These works address mainly executable code, interfaces and packages. In agent skills, behavior is specified in natural language, and copying between repositories does not reveal whether the content was executed, adapted or adopted independently.

Our study is close to the cloning literature in that it identifies identical contents by hash and counts where they appear. Unlike a count of repositories, we use the number of distinct **owners**, to separate diffusion among developers from duplication by a single account. The measure excludes modified copies and semantic equivalences; therefore, *reuse* is here an operational term, implying no quality, usefulness or adoption.

## 2.2 Agent skills and prompt artifacts

Research on LLMs with tool use studies selection, planning and execution of calls \cite{schick2023toolformer,shen2023hugginggpt,yao2023react,qin2023toolllm}. An agent skill differs from these protocols in being a persistent artifact, hosted in repositories, that describes procedures and conventions to guide the agent \cite{agentskills_spec}. GitSkills provides a large-scale corpus with contents, occurrences and metadata \cite{gitskills2027}. Other studies analyze specification and packaging defects \cite{zhang2026keepskillsreuse}, the effect of skills on real software engineering tasks \cite{han2026sweskillsbench} and the evolution of prompts in applications \cite{tafreshipour2025prompting}. None of them asks whether observable characteristics of a skill and its context are associated with diffusion across owners, nor examines how much the construction of the corpus affects this question.

## 2.3 Validation, leakage and construction bias

When observations from the same project, organization or entity appear in both training and evaluation, a model may memorize the group instead of learning relations that generalize \cite{kaufman2012leakage}. The literature recommends grouping dependent units in cross-validation \cite{saeb2017need,roberts2017cross}, and nested validation separates model selection from error estimation. Models may also exploit incidental regularities that do not represent the phenomenon \cite{geirhos2020shortcut,lapuschkin2019unmasking}. In mined data there is a variant: attributes produced by the collection or deduplication procedure itself may depend on the target by construction, without any column being derived from it. The choice of the representative of each content in GitSkills is an example. We treat this risk as a bias to be audited, and not merely as generic leakage, and adopt owner-grouped validation, a per-owner cap, hypotheses fixed before the holdout and confirmation on disjoint data.

## 2.4 Modeling, explainability and research gap

We model cross-owner reuse (binary) and `log1p(owner_count)` (a long-tailed count, evaluated by RMSLE), with six families of distinct inductive biases, with and without nested hyperparameter search. Explainability describes how the model combines attributes, but does not turn associations into causal effects; permutation importance depends on the model and is shared among correlated variables \cite{molnar2020interpretable,shmueli2010explain,breiman2001statistical}. Our contribution is empirical and methodological for the analyzed corpus: to audit the representative bias, to redefine the target in terms of owners, and to estimate, with a pre-specified protocol and a new holdout, how much signal remains. We do not intend to establish an exhaustive gap in the literature nor to demonstrate causality.
